About

State legislatures across the U.S. are actively writing laws governing algorithmic content feeds: how platforms rank and recommend content, what controls users and parents can exercise, how platforms handle minors, and what data access rights exist. This tracker, a joint project of Georgetown University's Department of Computer Science and Communication, Culture & Technology Program, catalogues that legislation and classifies each bill by how it would affect what users see across all 50 states. Learn more about the methodology and research team.

Georgetown University  ·  Research Documentation

About This Tracker

A legislative monitoring tool for studying how governments regulate algorithmic content feeds — built in support of the User Controls for Algorithmic Recommendation Systems project.

The Project

This tracker is part of "User Controls for Algorithmic Recommendation Systems," a research project at Georgetown University conducted jointly by the Department of Computer Science and the Communication, Culture & Technology Program. The project examines how users interact with algorithmic content feeds and what regulatory and design interventions could give them more meaningful control over what they see.

The project has two components. The first is user research, examining how people understand and engage with algorithmic recommendations. The second is legislative and policy analysis: mapping what governments are doing, or attempting to do, to regulate algorithmic feeds. Findings from both components will inform policy recommendations. This tracker supports the legislative and policy analysis component.

The tracker was built by Joel Piels (jp2035@georgetown.edu). The broader research team includes Dr. Meg Leta Jones, Dr. Elissa Redmiles, Dr. Eric Zeng, and Avand Lakmazaheri. Questions about the tracker or the research can be directed to Joel.

This research is supported by the Fritz Fellows Program at Georgetown University.

Research Question

The tracker is built around a single question: does a given bill materially affect what a user sees in their algorithmic feed?

The scope is deliberately narrow. Bills that mention social media or artificial intelligence in passing are not the target. The tracker looks specifically for legislation that would change how content is selected, ranked, or presented to users — through user controls, platform design requirements, transparency mandates, or restrictions on algorithmic personalization.

Coverage

The tracker currently covers all 50 U.S. state legislatures. Federal legislation (119th Congress) and comparative frameworks from the United Kingdom and Australia are tracked separately and will be integrated in a future phase.

How Bills Are Collected

Bills are sourced from LegiScan, a national legislative data service that aggregates bill text and metadata from all 50 state legislatures. The tracker runs 26 keyword searches against the full text of active bills, using phrases such as "algorithmic transparency," "social media recommendation," "user control algorithm," and "automated decision," across every active session in every state.

Every bill returned by those searches is scored by an automated classifier. Bills that score above a minimum threshold are stored; those containing no topic-relevant language are discarded. The system errs toward broader collection, since missing a relevant bill is a worse outcome than reviewing an irrelevant one.

The tracker runs at least once a month. When a bill has not changed since the previous run, the full bill details are not re-fetched. Bills that are new or updated are retrieved in full and re-classified.

How Bills Are Classified

Every stored bill is assigned one of six tiers based on whatever text LegiScan provides. Titles are always available; descriptions are present for most bills but not all. When a description is absent, classification falls back to the title alone.

DIRECT The bill title contains language directly describing feed-layer legislation: "algorithmic transparency," "chronological feed," "social media recommendation."
HIGH The bill substantially regulates algorithmic curation. Direct-match language appears in the description, or platform and algorithm terms appear alongside governance language, or youth-focused language is combined with platform references.
MEDIUM Privacy terms appear alongside platform or algorithm keywords. The bill is likely feed-adjacent but not primarily focused on feed mechanics.
LOW Platform or algorithm keywords are present without governance, youth, or privacy combinations. A weak signal that may be tangential.
MINIMAL Only privacy keywords are present, with no platform or algorithm signal. Unlikely to affect feed mechanics directly.
AMBIGUOUS The bill title is an acronym act name (such as "SAFE Act" or "KIDS Act") with no descriptive keywords and no description available. Classification is not possible from the available text; these bills are set aside for manual review.

Bills with no topic-relevant language at all are discarded before storage. This eliminates the bulk of false positives from broad keyword searches.

Deeper Review

For bills classified Direct, High, or Medium, the tracker fetches the full bill text and runs a two-part review.

The first part is a static analysis: a set of pattern-matching rules that scan the bill text and tag it against the research taxonomy, identifying what type of content control the bill creates, what mechanism it uses, and what platforms it covers.

The second part is an AI-assisted review using a large language model (Claude, via Anthropic's API). The bill text is submitted with a structured prompt asking the model to answer the research question directly. The model assigns a tier, classifies the bill against the taxonomy, and provides a rationale citing specific statutory language. Results are validated against the taxonomy before being stored.

Where AI review data is available, it appears on the bill detail view alongside the automated tier. The Analytics tab shows coverage and distribution across the full taxonomy.

Classification Taxonomy

Reviewed bills are classified along three dimensions.

Content Control Type captures what kind of control or restriction the bill creates: user controls, parental controls, age-gating, default settings, ranking and recommendation control, sensitive category suppression, data privacy, opt-out rights, opt-in requirements, or content removal. A single bill can have multiple types.

Mechanism describes how the bill achieves its effect: design and defaults, verification, notice and choice, reporting, labeling, in-feed notifications, or data access.

Scope identifies what the bill covers: social media platforms specifically, general online platforms broadly, or devices.

Companion Bills

When a bill is introduced in both chambers of a legislature, both versions are stored and linked. They appear together as a single entry in the dashboard, displayed side by side to allow status comparison across chambers.

Using This Site

The public version of this tracker is read-only. No action taken here modifies the underlying database. To run the tracker, adjust classifications, or contribute to the database, the program must be run in a local environment. The source code is maintained in a private repository; access is available on request.

Each bill card shows when its metadata was last fetched. Bills that have been through a recent tracker run will reflect a current date; lower-priority bills that were not re-fetched will show an older one. That difference is intentional and reflects the tracker's update logic.

Limitations

The tracker does not take a position on any legislation. It does not assess whether a bill is sound policy, likely to pass, or constitutionally viable. It is a data collection and classification tool, not an evaluative one.

The automated scoring system has known failure modes. Omnibus bills sometimes score highly because they contain relevant language in one narrow section among many unrelated provisions. The deeper review layer catches most of these cases, but human judgment remains necessary for rigorous analysis. Tiers and classifications should be read as research signals.

Coverage is also bounded by LegiScan's data and the tracker's keyword set. Bills using terminology outside that vocabulary, or structured in ways the searches do not capture, will not appear here.
